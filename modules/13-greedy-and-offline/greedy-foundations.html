Greedy Foundations

A greedy choice is legal only with an exchange argument or a matroid / stay-ahead proof — "it looks locally good" is not a proof.

Easy 28 min read Prerequisites: Problem-Solving Framework, Sorting & Comparators greedy exchange stay-ahead P0

Why this exists

You are given a list of meetings, each with a start time and a finish time, and you must pack as many as possible into one room so that no two overlap. Trying every subset is perfectly correct: with twenty meetings that is about a million subsets, and with a hundred thousand meetings it is not an option the judge will wait for. A tempting shortcut is "always take the meeting that starts first", but one long meeting that opens at time 0 and closes at noon then blocks every short meeting that could have fitted after nine. The technique on this page is the proof that tells you which shortcut is legal.

The subset scan costs 2n times a linear overlap check. At n = 40 that is already a trillion steps, and dynamic programming on "which meetings I have used" is the same exponential unless you invent a small numeric state. What a legal greedy buys is a sort plus one pass: each of the n meetings is compared during the sort at log n cost, then scanned once, which is about 1.7 × 106 operations at n = 105. That budget only works when you can prove the first choice you make already appears in some optimal answer.

The proof is an exchange argument, said in words before any symbol appears: take any optimal answer, and if its first choice is not mine, swap that first choice for mine without making the answer worse. After the swap you still have a legal schedule, it is still optimal, and it now agrees with you on the first pick. Repeat on whatever is left. If every swap stays feasible and never loses quality, your whole sequence is optimal. Stay-ahead is the cousin: after k picks, your k-th event is already at least as good as anyone else's, so the last step is optimal by itself.

In a real problem statement the signal is a request to pick, schedule, or order items sitting next to limits such as n ≤ 105, with no extra weight on each item. That combination rules out subset enumeration and usually rules out DP. The same paragraph covers Huffman merging, Kruskal's lightest-edge rule, and deadline scheduling. Coin change is the counterexample you should name: US denominations happen to work, but the set {1, 3, 4} making 6 prefers 3+3 over the greedy 4+1+1, so the swap can lose and you write DP instead.

Take any optimal answer and swap its first choice for mine without making it worse; if that swap is always legal, induction says the whole greedy sequence is optimal. Stay-ahead is the same idea on prefixes: after k picks you are already no worse than any other feasible k-set.

Recognise this pattern

Say yes when you see

  • An ordering (by start, by ratio, by deadline) suggests itself and a swap does not hurt
  • Matroid flavour: independent sets, Kruskal, scheduling with unit jobs
  • "Take the smallest / earliest / highest-ratio next" in the statement
  • You can prove a stay-ahead invariant on prefixes of the schedule
  • Counterexamples for nearby rules come easily, so this particular rule might be the right one

Not this pattern when

  • Overlapping subproblems with a numeric state → DP
  • Need a global matching / flow → not a local choice
  • Arbitrary coin systems, unbounded knapsack → DP
  • The swap can make a later constraint infeasible → greedy is lying

Core idea and the invariant

Call the greedy sequence G and some optimal sequence O, both written in the order of the sort key. Walk them from the left until the first index i where they disagree: G took item g and O took item o. Build a new sequence O' by putting g into O in place of o (or in place of whichever later O-item conflicts with g). The exchange argument is the claim that O' is still feasible and that cost(O') is no worse than cost(O) — for a maximum-count problem, |O'| ≥ |O|. Then O' is optimal and agrees with G one step longer.

Stay-ahead names a different pair of quantities. After k choices, let tG(k) be the time (or load, or finish) of greedy's k-th event, and let tO(k) be the same number for any other feasible k-set. The invariant is tG(k) ≤ tO(k) (or the reverse, depending on the measure). Because the next greedy pick is the locally best remaining item, the inequality survives one more step. When the sequences end, greedy is at least as good as O, so it is optimal. Dijkstra's extracted distances and Huffman's merged weights are stay-ahead arguments of this shape.

Walk the six activities (1,4), (3,5), (0,6), (5,7), (8,9), (5,9) after sorting by finish time. Greedy takes index 0 because it finishes first, at time 4. Indices 1 and 2 start before 4 so they are skipped; index 3 starts at 5 and is taken; index 4 starts at 8 and is taken; index 5 overlaps. The set {0, 3, 4} has size 3. Any optimal that skipped 0 must have taken some later job that overlaps 0; swapping that job for 0 frees the timeline no later than before, so the count does not drop, and the rest of the proof is induction.

If a swap that aligns the next greedy choice with some optimal solution never worsens that solution, the greedy prefix is contained in some optimal solution.

In plain words, take any optimal answer, swap its first choice for mine, and check that you did not make it worse — if that sentence is true, you may code the sort.

Interview sentence: "I take any optimal answer and swap its first choice for mine without making it worse."

The exchange in one breath

Take any optimal answer. Swap its first choice for mine. If the swap never makes that answer worse, induction finishes the proof. If you cannot finish that paragraph on the whiteboard, do not ship the greedy.

Nearby keys have counters

Earliest start and shortest duration look locally smart and fail on tiny instances. Coin change on {1,3,4} for 6 is the other classic: greedy takes 4 and then two 1s, while 3+3 is better, so the swap can lose.

Proof first, then the sort

Algorithm

  1. Name the first choice and the key. Say out loud which item is taken first and by which number (finish time, density, deadline), because that sentence is the thing the exchange has to defend.
  2. Sort by that key, with a justified tie-break. The order is the candidate greedy sequence; a tie-break you cannot explain is usually a sign that the key is still fuzzy.
  3. Scan once and take every still-feasible item. After each take, update the resource you spent (last finish, remaining capacity) so the next check is a constant-time comparison.
  4. Write the exchange before you code. Take any optimal answer, swap its first choice for yours, and check that the swap does not make that answer worse; if the sentence stalls, stop.
  5. Hunt a counterexample for the next-most-tempting key. Earliest start and shortest duration look locally smart; a two-meeting instance that breaks them is what you recite when the interviewer asks "why this key".
  6. Implement the scan only after the paragraph works. Greedy code is usually ten lines after the sort, which is exactly why a wrong key ships so easily and why the proof comes first.

Java implementation

Enumerate subsets. Only n ≤ 20.

JavaActivityBrute.java
public class ActivityBrute {
        static int best(int[] s, int[] f) {
            int n = s.length, ans = 0;
            for (int m = 0; m < (1 << n); m++) {
                int cnt = 0, ok = 1, last = -1;
                for (int i = 0; i < n && ok == 1; i++) if (((m >> i) & 1) == 1) {
                    if (s[i] < last) ok = 0;
                    else { last = f[i]; cnt++; }
                }
                if (ok == 1) ans = Math.max(ans, cnt);
            }
            return ans;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(best(new int[] {1, 3, 0, 5, 8, 5}, new int[] {4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 9}));
        }
        // Input : classic 6 activities
        // Output: 3
    }

Sort by finish time, take if start ≥ last finish.

JavaActivityGreedy.java
import java.util.*;
    public class ActivityGreedy {
        static int pick(int[] s, int[] f) {
            int n = s.length;
            Integer[] id = new Integer[n];
            for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) id[i] = i;
            Arrays.sort(id, Comparator.comparingInt(i -> f[i]));
            int cnt = 0, last = -1;
            for (int i : id) if (s[i] >= last) { last = f[i]; cnt++; }
            return cnt;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(pick(new int[] {1, 3, 0, 5, 8, 5}, new int[] {4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 9}));
        }
        // Input : same six
        // Output: 3
    }

Complexity

Time O(n log n) Space O(n)

Each of the n items is compared during the sort, and a comparison-based sort costs log n per item, which is n log n operations in total. The later scan touches each item once and does a constant-time feasibility check, so it is absorbed. Putting real numbers in: at n = 105 the sort is about 1.7 × 106 comparisons and finishes in milliseconds.

The brute alternative enumerates 2n subsets and spends a linear scan on each, which is already a trillion steps at n = 40 and is why the exchange has to exist. When the exchange fails you fall back to DP whose cost is whatever the state space is, not this n log n bound.

Edge cases and pitfalls

  • Sorting by start time
    Earliest-start looks right because it fills the room immediately, but a long early meeting then blocks every short one that could have fitted later, so the count can drop by one on a three-meeting instance.
    Sort by earliest finish, not earliest start. Test it on (0,10), (1,2), (3,4): start-time greedy takes one, finish-time greedy takes two.
  • Skipping the proof because the sample passed
    Coin change on {1,3,4} for 6 looks like the US-coin habit, so greedy takes 4 then two 1s and reports 3 coins, which matches a wrong mental model of "largest first always works".
    If you cannot finish the swap paragraph, write DP. The counter is 3+3, two coins; recite it when someone says "coins are greedy".
  • Wrong tie-break
    Equal finish times make any pick look interchangeable for the unweighted count, so taking the later start still passes samples, while the weighted version silently needs DP and a reconstruction that this greedy cannot give.
    Unweighted count: any tie-break is fine. Weighted activity: this greedy is the wrong algorithm, so switch to DP on sorted finishes.
  • Mutating the original index order
    Sorting the finish array in place and leaving starts unmoved looks like a harmless reorder, but it pairs each finish with the wrong start and the scan then accepts overlapping jobs.
    Sort an index array, or sort pairs (start, finish) together. Check that after the sort, s[id[i]] still belongs to f[id[i]].
  • Claiming Huffman / Kruskal without naming the structure
    "Because Kruskal is greedy" sounds like a reason in an interview, but it names an algorithm instead of a property, so a follow-up of "why does the swap not lose?" has nowhere to go.
    Say the exchange, the cut property, or "it is a matroid" in one sentence. The algorithm name is not the proof.

Interview follow-ups

Give a one-line exchange for earliest-finish.

Take any optimal answer and look at the first job in it that overlaps greedy's first pick. Greedy's pick finishes no later than that job, so swapping it in frees the timeline at least as early and the rest of the optimal schedule stays feasible. The count does not drop, so the new answer is still optimal and now agrees on the first choice.

When is coin greedy correct?

It is correct on canonical coin systems such as US denominations, where each coin is at least twice the next smaller one in a way that makes the swap never lose. The proof is not obvious and is easy to get wrong. In a contest, assume DP unless the statement names a canonical set or the limits are huge and the coins are exactly 1, 5, 10, 25.

Matroid in one sentence?

A matroid is a family of independent sets that is closed under taking subsets and has an exchange property: you can always grow the smaller of two independent sets by stealing an element from the larger. Kruskal is greedy on the graphic matroid (forests of a graph). Weighted greedy is optimal on every matroid, which is why "it is a matroid" is a complete interview reason.

Stay-ahead vs exchange?

Stay-ahead compares prefixes: after k picks, greedy's k-th event is already no worse than any other feasible k-set, and the last step is then optimal by itself. Exchange rewrites a full optimal solution until it matches greedy. Both are induction. Use whichever paragraph is shorter for the problem in front of you; Dijkstra and Huffman are usually stay-ahead, activity selection is usually exchange.
