Manacher's Algorithm

Manacher computes the longest palindromic radius at every centre in O(n) by mirroring inside a rightmost palindrome box — the palindrome cousin of the Z-function.

Hard 32 min read Prerequisites: Z-Function, Two Pointers Manacher palindrome P2

Why this exists

You are given a string and you want the longest slice that reads the same forwards and backwards, or you want every such slice counted. The honest method sits at each of the 2n-1 possible centres — one on every character and one in every gap — and walks outwards while the two sides match. That is correct, and a string of all the same letter makes every walk run the full length, which is about n2 comparisons. At n = 105 that is ten billion steps, and the judge will not wait.

Manacher's observation is that a palindrome is its own mirror, so radii you have already measured on the left of a big palindrome are radii you can copy on the right. Keep the rightmost palindrome found so far as a box [L, R] with centre c. For a new centre i that still sits inside that box, its mirror about c is the index j = 2c - i, and the palindrome already sitting at j is a picture of the palindrome sitting at i, at least until you hit the box edge. You copy that radius, clip it so it does not claim past R, and only then walk characters if the copy ran out of known ground.

That is the Z-function skeleton with a palindrome in place of a prefix-copy. The usual implementation inserts a border and a # between every letter so odd and even palindromes of the original string become ordinary odd palindromes of one longer string T. The array d[i] is then the radius at centre i in T, and the original-string length is d[i] - 1. LC 5 is the interview prompt; contest follow-ups count palindromic slices or ask for the longest palindromic prefix.

In a real statement the signal is n ≤ 106 together with "longest palindromic substring" or "count palindromic substrings". If the string is mutable, this static array is the wrong tool. If the question is about subsequences rather than contiguous slices, this page does not apply: that is an O(n2) DP. If you need every distinct palindrome as a node with its own suffix link, you want an eertree, not Manacher.

Inside the rightmost palindrome, smaller palindromes mirror: the radius at i starts as min(d[2c-i], R-i), you clip so you do not invent characters past the box, and you only extend when that copy hits the edge. The right end R never moves left, so the extra comparisons stay linear — the same amortised argument as the Z-box.

Recognise this pattern

Say yes when you see

Core idea and the invariant

First build a string T that has a unique left border ^, a unique right border $, and a # sitting in every gap, including the ends. The sample aba becomes ^#a#b#a#$. Every palindrome of the original string, odd or even, is now an odd palindrome of T: even ones of s are centred on a #. You need an array d of length |T|, plus a centre c and a right end R for the rightmost palindrome found so far. The value d[i] is how far you can walk from i in T and still have a palindrome, counting the centre itself, so a lone centre starts at 1.

The mirror step is the load-bearing idea, so sit with why it cannot lose a palindrome. Suppose the rightmost palindrome is T[L..R] with centre c, which means T[c-k] = T[c+k] for every k up to the radius. A new centre i with c < i < R has a mirror j = 2c - i on the left of c, the same distance away. Because the big palindrome equals its reverse, a walk of length k around j that stays inside [L, R] is the same walk, letter for letter, around i. So the palindrome already measured at j is a palindrome at i as well, at least until one of those walks would step past R (or past L, which is the same event by symmetry). That is why the copy is d[i] = min(d[j], R-i) and not a blind d[j]: claiming more than R-i would invent characters you have never compared at this alignment.

You still have to extend after the copy, for the same reason Z extends after clipping. If d[j] is strictly smaller than the remaining box, the palindrome at j ended for a real mismatch, and by the mirror that same mismatch sits at i, so you must not grow. If d[j] reaches exactly to the box edge, the palindrome at j was clipped by the box rather than by a mismatch, and the next pair of characters around i is unknown: they might still match, and the while is what finds out. After extending, if i + d[i] now past R, this palindrome is the new rightmost box and you move c and R. The right end never decreases. Every successful extend comparison increases R, so those comparisons total at most |T|, and a failed extend happens at most once per centre. The whole fill is linear, just like Z.

Walk aba on T = ^#a#b#a#$. The first letter-centre is the first a; you expand to #a# and store radius 2, and that small palindrome is the live box. The centre at b is outside that box, so you start from 1 and expand through the whole string: #a#b#a# is a palindrome of radius 4, and the box now covers everything. The second a sits inside that box; its mirror is the first a, whose radius is 2, and the remaining box is also 2, so you copy 2 and the extend cannot grow. The longest original slice has length d-1 = 3 at centre b, which is aba. An even palindrome such as aa would have shown up as a large radius on the # sitting between the two letters.

The rightmost palindrome T[L..R] with centre c is a mirror, so every smaller palindrome inside it has a copy:

for i in (c, R),  d[i] ≥ min(d[2c-i], R-i), and d[i] equals that minimum unless the copy hits the box edge and the next pair still matches.

In plain words, you never invent characters past the known palindrome, you never throw away a palindrome the mirror already proved, and the only centres that cost more than a couple of array reads are the ones that push R right. Interview sentence: "I copy the mirrored radius, clip to the box, then extend; R only grows, so Manacher is linear."

Clip to the box or you invent a palindrome

Copying d[2c-i] without the min(..., R-i) looks right because the two centres are mirrors. The palindrome at the mirror may stick out past the left edge of the big box; the corresponding walk on the right would then step past R, where the big palindrome no longer promises anything. Those extra characters can disagree, and you have just stored a radius that is too big.

The sentinels are bounds checks you do not write

^ and $ are two characters that appear nowhere else, so the extend loop hits a guaranteed mismatch at each end instead of an ArrayIndexOutOfBoundsException. The # characters are what give even palindromes of s a centre of their own. Test the decode on a, aa, and aba before you trust (centre - best) / 2.

Why the mirror cannot skip a palindrome

Anything the expand-around-centre method would have found at i while staying inside [L, R] is, letter for letter, the expand it already found at the mirror j. Anything it would have found past R is exactly what the extend loop is about to try. There is no third kind of palindrome, so the copy-plus-clip-plus-extend sequence reports the same radius the quadratic method would have, just without redoing the inner work.

Mirror, clip, extend

Algorithm

  1. Build the sentinel string first. Write ^, then # and each letter, then a closing #$. The two unique borders stop the extend loop at the ends, and the hashes give even palindromes of s a centre of their own.
  2. Allocate the radius array and an empty box. Let d have length |T|, and start with c = 0 and R = 0 so the first real centre has nothing to mirror yet.
  3. Copy the mirrored radius when i sits inside the box. The mirror is 2*c - i. If i < R, set d[i] = min(d[mirror], R-i); otherwise start at 1. The clip is what stops you inventing characters past the known palindrome.
  4. Extend with fresh comparisons. While the two characters at i - d[i] and i + d[i] agree, increment. This is the only place new characters are read, and it is what grows a palindrome that the mirror only proved up to the box edge.
  5. Move the box only when this centre wins the right end. If i + d[i] > R, set c = i and R = i + d[i]. Charging every successful extend to a rightward move of R is the linear-time argument.
  6. Decode back into the original string. With this construction the palindrome length in s is d[i] - 1, and the start index is (i - d[i]) / 2. Confirm the arithmetic on a, aa, and aba before you ship it.

Java implementation

Expand around 2n-1 centres. O(n^2).

JavaPalExpand.java
public class PalExpand {
        static String longest(String s) {
            int n = s.length(), bestL = 0, bestR = 0;
            for (int c = 0; c < n; c++) {
                int[] odd = expand(s, c, c), even = expand(s, c, c + 1);
                if (odd[1] - odd[0] > bestR - bestL) { bestL = odd[0]; bestR = odd[1]; }
                if (even[1] - even[0] > bestR - bestL) { bestL = even[0]; bestR = even[1]; }
            }
            return s.substring(bestL, bestR);
        }
        static int[] expand(String s, int l, int r) {
            while (l >= 0 && r < s.length() && s.charAt(l) == s.charAt(r)) { l--; r++; }
            return new int[] {l + 1, r};
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(longest("babad"));
        }
        // Input : babad
        // Output: bab
    }

Linear radii on the sentinel string. Returns the longest piece of s.

JavaManacher.java
public class Manacher {
        static String longest(String s) {
            StringBuilder sb = new StringBuilder("^");
            for (int i = 0; i < s.length(); i++) sb.append('#').append(s.charAt(i));
            sb.append("#$");
            char[] t = sb.toString().toCharArray();
            int m = t.length;
            int[] d = new int[m];
            int c = 0, r = 0, best = 0, centre = 0;
            for (int i = 1; i < m - 1; i++) {
                if (i < r) d[i] = Math.min(d[2 * c - i], r - i);
                else d[i] = 1;
                while (t[i - d[i]] == t[i + d[i]]) d[i]++;
                if (i + d[i] > r) { c = i; r = i + d[i]; }
                if (d[i] > best) { best = d[i]; centre = i; }
            }
            int start = (centre - best) / 2;
            return s.substring(start, start + best - 1);
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(longest("babad"));
            System.out.println(longest("cbbd"));
        }
        // Input : babad, then cbbd
        // Output: bab
        //         bb
    }

Count palindromic substrings: each radius contributes d[i]/2 centres in s.

JavaManacherCount.java
public class ManacherCount {
        static int count(String s) {
            StringBuilder sb = new StringBuilder("^");
            for (int i = 0; i < s.length(); i++) sb.append('#').append(s.charAt(i));
            sb.append("#$");
            char[] t = sb.toString().toCharArray();
            int[] d = new int[t.length];
            int c = 0, r = 0, ans = 0;
            for (int i = 1; i < t.length - 1; i++) {
                if (i < r) d[i] = Math.min(d[2 * c - i], r - i);
                else d[i] = 1;
                while (t[i - d[i]] == t[i + d[i]]) d[i]++;
                if (i + d[i] > r) { c = i; r = i + d[i]; }
                ans += d[i] / 2;
            }
            return ans;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(count("aaa"));
        }
        // Input : aaa
        // Output: 6
    }

Complexity

Time O(n) Space O(n)

There are |T| = 2n+3 centres and the mirror copy is a constant amount of arithmetic at each one. The only loop that can run more than once per centre is the extend. Every successful extend comparison increases R by at least one, and R never decreases, so successful extends total at most |T|. A failed extend comparison happens at most once per centre after the copied radius is installed, which is another |T| comparisons.

Putting real numbers in: at n = 106 the sentinel string has about two million centres and you do a few million character reads, which is a few milliseconds. Expand-around-centre on a string of all as is about n2 comparisons, 1012 at that size, and that is the runtime the mirror is designed to avoid. Hashing plus binary search finds only the single longest palindrome in O(n log n) and does not fill every radius.

Edge cases and pitfalls

  • Forgetting sentinels ^ and $
    Leaving the ends of T as ordinary characters looks fine on a sample whose longest palindrome sits in the middle. The first time a palindrome reaches an end, the extend loop reads past the array and throws, or it wraps into a coincidental match.
    Put two unique borders on T, or write explicit bounds checks in the while. Test a single-character string and a string that is itself a palindrome.
  • d counting vs expand-by-1
    Mixing "d is the length in T" with "d is the number of extra steps past the centre" looks like a documentation issue. The start-index formula then shifts by one and you return a neighbour of the real answer, which still looks palindromic on short samples.
    Pick one convention and keep it. The listing uses d = 1 meaning just the centre, then extends while the next pair matches. Check the decode on a, aa, and aba together.
  • Using substring inside the extend loop
    Comparing T.substring(...) in the inner loop looks like the cleanest way to say "these two sides match". On older JDKs each call copies, and even on new ones you have turned a linear scan into a quadratic allocation storm.
    Work on a char[] and compare two indices. The inner loop must be two array reads and an increment, nothing else.
  • Even palindromes without '#'
    Expanding only around letters looks complete because those are the centres you can see. Every even palindrome of s, including abba and aa, is then invisible, and LC 5 returns a shorter odd piece.
    Build the sentinel string, or keep two radius arrays dOdd and dEven with the same box idea run twice. Test cbbd, whose answer is the even slice bb.
  • Decoding start index wrong
    The formula (centre - best) / 2 versus (centre - best + 1) / 2 depends on whether T starts with a hash, and both versions look plausible. One of them passes aba and fails aa.
    Derive the start once from T = ^#s0#s1#...#$ and lock it with tests on a, aa, and aba. Do not mix constructions from two blog posts.

Interview follow-ups

Why is this linear like Z?

The right end R is a potential. A mirror copy is a constant amount of work. Every successful extend comparison increases R, and R never decreases, so those comparisons total at most |T|. A failed extend happens at most once per centre. That is the same charging argument as the Z-box, with a palindrome in place of a prefix-copy. One centre might expand a long way, but it spends credit that is never spent again.

Can you support updates?

Not with a static d[]. Rebuilding after each change is O(n) and is fine if updates are rare. Online insertion of a character at the end is what an eertree (palindromic tree) is for. If the question is only "is this range a palindrome?" after point updates, a Fenwick or segment tree of polynomial hash coefficients can answer it, which is a heavier structure and a different page.

Longest palindromic subsequence vs substring?

A subsequence does not have to be contiguous, so the mirror box does not apply. The usual answer is an O(n2) DP, or LCS of the string with its reverse. Manacher reports contiguous slices only. In an interview, ask which word they said before you reach for this page.

How do you list all palindromic substrings without O(n^2) memory?

The O(n) radii already represent every palindromic slice: a centre with radius d in T contributes d/2 palindromes of the original string. Iterate centres and emit the ranges on demand. Materialising every slice as a String is what blows the memory, not the algorithm that found them.
