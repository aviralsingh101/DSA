Suffix Array & LCP

A suffix array is the permutation of starting indices in lexicographic order; Kasai then fills the LCP array in O(n) and string problems become range-min on LCP.

Hard 32 min read Prerequisites: Sorting & Comparators, Sparse Table & RMQ suffix array LCP P2

Why this exists

You are given a string and a question about its slices: how many distinct ones are there, what is the longest slice that appears twice, what is the longest slice two strings share, or what is the k-th slice in dictionary order. Every slice of a string is a prefix of one of its suffixes, so if you sort the suffixes you have sorted every slice. The obvious sort compares those suffixes with compareTo, which can read O(n) characters per comparison and costs something like n2 log n in the worst case. At n = 105 that is not a contest-legal runtime.

A suffix array is just the permutation of starting indices in that sorted order. Once you have it, the useful second array is the LCP array: lcp[i] is how many leading characters suffix sa[i-1] and suffix sa[i] share. The longest common prefix of any two suffixes is then the minimum LCP on the interval of ranks between them, which a sparse table answers in constant time. Distinct-substring count, longest repeated slice, and the k-th slice all fall out of that picture without ever listing the slices.

Building the permutation in O(n log n) uses doubling: at step k you already know the order of every block of length 2k, and you sort pairs of those blocks to get length 2k+1. Filling the LCP array looks like it should be another O(n2) of character walking, but Kasai's observation kills that: if suffix i shares h characters with its sorted predecessor, then suffix i+1 shares at least h-1 with its predecessor. You walk the suffixes in text order, not sorted order, and you carry that leftover height down.

In a Java contest this pair plus a sparse table on LCP is the structure you actually submit. Suffix trees and suffix automata are more powerful and more code. If the question is one pattern in one text, do not build this: write KMP. If characters are appended online, a suffix automaton is the better fit. The usual limit is n ≤ 4 × 105 for the doubling-plus-Java-sort build on this page.

Sort the suffixes once; the LCP of any two of them is the range-minimum of the adjacent-LCP array between their ranks. Kasai fills that adjacent array in linear time by walking the suffixes in text order and reusing h-1 characters of height, which is the observation that stops the fill from going quadratic.

Core idea and the invariant

You need a permutation sa and a rank array r. Initially sa[i] = i and r[i] is the first character of suffix i. At round k (with k a power of two) two suffixes are compared by the pair (r[i], r[i+k]), using -1 when the second half runs off the end so a shorter leftover loses. After you sort sa by those pairs you reassign ranks: equal pairs keep the same new rank, a different pair gets the next integer. Swap the rank buffers and stop early once every rank is unique. After log n rounds you have compared prefixes of length n, which is a full suffix comparison.

Kasai then fills lcp without sorting anything else. Build the inverse inv[sa[i]] = i so you can ask "where in the sorted order does suffix i sit?". Walk the starting indices in text order, not sorted order, and keep an integer h for the leftover height. For suffix i, look at the suffix that sits immediately before it in sa, and extend h while the two still match. Write that value into lcp[inv[i]], then if h is positive decrement it before you move to suffix i+1. That decrement is the lemma: deleting the first character of a match of length h leaves a match of length h-1 as a lower bound on the next pair.

Why the decrement cannot go wrong is the same kind of "we never lose a match" argument as KMP's fallback. Suffix i and its sorted predecessor share h characters, so the suffixes i+1 and "predecessor plus one" share the last h-1 of those. The actual sorted predecessor of suffix i+1 is some suffix that is at least as large as "predecessor plus one", and therefore its LCP with i+1 is at least that leftover h-1 (it can be longer, which is what the extend loop finds). You never restart from zero except when h was already zero or when suffix i is the smallest in the array. Because h grows by at most one per successful comparison and drops by at most one per index, the whole fill is linear.

Walk banana. The six suffixes, already sorted, start at indices 5 (a), 3 (ana), 1 (anana), 0 (banana), 4 (na), 2 (nana), so sa = [5, 3, 1, 0, 4, 2]. Adjacent LCPs are 1, 3, 0, 0, 2. The longest repeated slice is the maximum of those, which is 3, the string ana starting at 1 and at 3. The number of distinct slices is the number of prefixes of suffixes, n(n+1)/2 = 21, minus the ones the LCPs counted twice, which is 21 - 6 = 15. Any later "LCP of suffix 3 and suffix 1" is the range-minimum of the adjacent values strictly between their ranks, here just 3.

The suffix array is the lex order of starting indices, and adjacent LCPs generate every pairwise LCP:

sa[i] = start of the i-th smallest suffix,  lcp[i] = LCP(sa[i-1], sa[i]),  LCP(sa[i], sa[j]) = min(lcp[i+1..j]) for i < j

In plain words, two suffixes share everything their neighbours on the path between them share, and the shortest of those neighbour-shares is the answer. Interview sentence: "I sort suffixes by doubling, Kasai walks text order with h = max(h-1, 0), and any LCP is an RMQ on that array."

Forgetting k-- turns Kasai quadratic

Restarting the character walk from 0 at every suffix still produces the right LCP array, so the bug hides behind a correct sample. You have thrown away the lemma and the fill is O(n2) on a string of all as. The line after you write lcp[inv[i]] has to be if (h > 0) h--;.

Out-of-range rank is -1, not 0

A suffix that has no second half must lose to every suffix that still has characters there. Treating the missing half as rank 0 ties it with a real character that mapped to 0, and the ranks never become unique. The listing uses -1, strictly smaller than any character rank.

Algorithm

  1. Map characters to small ranks. Replace the alphabet with integers 1..sigma and treat a missing second half as -1, never as 0. This is what keeps two suffixes that run off the end from tying forever.
  2. Double the compared length each round. For k = 1, 2, 4, ... sort the current permutation by the pair (rank[i], rank[i+k]) and write a new rank array in that order. Equal pairs keep the same rank; a different pair gets the next integer.
  3. Stop once every rank is unique. When r[sa[n-1]] == n-1 the permutation is a full suffix order and further rounds do no work. At worst you need log n rounds.
  4. Fill LCP with Kasai in text order. Build inv[sa[i]] = i, start h = 0, and for each starting index i extend against the sorted predecessor, write lcp[inv[i]], then decrement h if it is positive. That decrement is the linear-time lemma.
  5. Put a sparse table on the LCP array. Any later "how much do suffix x and suffix y share?" is a range-minimum strictly between their ranks, which is constant time after an O(n log n) table.
  6. Answer the actual string question from those two arrays. Distinct slices are n(n+1)/2 - sum lcp, the longest repeat is max lcp, two-string LCS is the max LCP across a separator, and a pattern search is a binary search on sa.

Java implementation

Sort suffixes with String.compareTo. O(n^2 log n).

JavaSaBrute.java
import java.util.*;
    public class SaBrute {
        static int[] sa(String s) {
            Integer[] idx = new Integer[s.length()];
            for (int i = 0; i < idx.length; i++) idx[i] = i;
            Arrays.sort(idx, (a, b) -> s.substring(a).compareTo(s.substring(b)));
            int[] sa = new int[idx.length];
            for (int i = 0; i < idx.length; i++) sa[i] = idx[i];
            return sa;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(Arrays.toString(sa("banana")));
        }
        // Input : banana
        // Output: [5, 3, 1, 0, 4, 2]
    }

O(n log^2 n) doubling with Java sort. Fine for n ≤ 1e5.

JavaSuffixArray.java
import java.util.*;
    public class SuffixArray {
        static int[] sa(String s) {
            int n = s.length();
            Integer[] sa = new Integer[n];
            int[] r = new int[n], nr = new int[n];
            for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) { sa[i] = i; r[i] = s.charAt(i); }
            for (int k = 1; k < n; k <<= 1) {
                final int kk = k;
                Arrays.sort(sa, (a, b) -> {
                    if (r[a] != r[b]) return Integer.compare(r[a], r[b]);
                    int ra = a + kk < n ? r[a + kk] : -1;
                    int rb = b + kk < n ? r[b + kk] : -1;
                    return Integer.compare(ra, rb);
                });
                nr[sa[0]] = 0;
                for (int i = 1; i < n; i++) {
                    int a = sa[i - 1], b = sa[i];
                    boolean same = r[a] == r[b]
                        && (a + kk < n ? r[a + kk] : -1) == (b + kk < n ? r[b + kk] : -1);
                    nr[b] = nr[a] + (same ? 0 : 1);
                }
                int[] tmp = r; r = nr; nr = tmp;
                if (r[sa[n - 1]] == n - 1) break;
            }
            int[] out = new int[n];
            for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) out[i] = sa[i];
            return out;
        }
        static int[] lcp(String s, int[] sa) {
            int n = s.length();
            int[] inv = new int[n], lcp = new int[n];
            for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) inv[sa[i]] = i;
            int k = 0;
            for (int i = 0; i < n; i++) {
                if (inv[i] == 0) { k = 0; continue; }
                int j = sa[inv[i] - 1];
                while (i + k < n && j + k < n && s.charAt(i + k) == s.charAt(j + k)) k++;
                lcp[inv[i]] = k;
                if (k > 0) k--;
            }
            return lcp;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            String s = "banana";
            int[] sa = sa(s);
            System.out.println(Arrays.toString(sa));
            System.out.println(Arrays.toString(lcp(s, sa)));
        }
        // Input : banana
        // Output: [5, 3, 1, 0, 4, 2]
        //         [0, 1, 3, 0, 0, 2]
    }

n(n+1)/2 minus sum of adjacent LCPs.

JavaDistinctSubstrings.java
public class DistinctSubstrings {
        static long distinct(int n, int[] lcp) {
            long all = (long) n * (n + 1) / 2;
            for (int x : lcp) all -= x;
            return all;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            int[] lcp = {0, 1, 3, 0, 0, 2};
            System.out.println(distinct(6, lcp));
        }
        // Input : banana LCPs
        // Output: 15
    }

Complexity

Time O(n log^2 n) this code; O(n log n) with radix; O(n) SA-IS Space O(n)

Doubling runs at most log n rounds. Each round sorts n pairs; Java Timsort is O(n log n) per round, so the build is O(n log2 n). Replacing that sort with a two-pass radix sort on ranks drops the extra log and is the usual next step when the same code TLEs. Kasai is a different argument: the height h grows by at most one per successful character comparison and drops by at most one per starting index, so the extend loop runs at most 2n times and the fill is strictly linear.

Putting real numbers in: at n = 105 you have about 17 sort rounds of a hundred thousand pairs, which is a few tens of millions of comparisons and fits in Java. At n = 4 × 105 the extra log starts to pinch and a radix sort, or SA-IS, is the safer submit. The naive substring sort is O(n2 log n), about 1011 character reads at the smaller size, which is the runtime this page exists to avoid.

Edge cases and pitfalls

  • Kasai forgetting k--
    Restarting the character walk from zero at every suffix still writes the correct LCP array, so the sample looks finished. You have thrown away the leftover-height lemma, and a string of all as makes the fill quadratic.
    After you write lcp[inv[i]], do if (h > 0) h--;. Time the fill on a string of a hundred thousand as; without the decrement it will miss the limit.
  • No sentinel / ties
    Two suffixes that share characters all the way to the end of the string compare equal if the missing half is treated as a real rank. Their ranks never split, doubling never finishes, and the permutation is not a total order.
    Treat an out-of-range second half as -1, strictly smaller than any character. That is the sentinel this build uses instead of appending a 0 to the string.
  • lcp[0] meaning
    Leaving lcp[0] = 0 unused looks harmless, and summing then subtracts an extra zero. A range-minimum between ranks 0 and 1 that accidentally reads lcp[0] as a real adjacent LCP reports 0 when the true share is lcp[1].
    The convention on this page is lcp[i] = LCP(sa[i-1], sa[i]) for i ≥ 1. A pairwise query between ranks i < j reads the minimum on lcp[i+1..j] and never lcp[i].
  • Building SA of s+t without a separator
    Concatenating two strings and sorting the suffixes looks like the standard longest-common-substring trick. A suffix can cross the join, so an LCP can mix the tail of the first string with the head of the second and report a slice that lives in neither input.
    Put a character smaller than both alphabets between them, the same idea as KMP's sentinel. Only accept an LCP whose two suffixes sit on opposite sides of that character.
  • Integer overflow on n(n+1)/2
    The distinct-substring formula is sitting there as a textbook identity, and n * (n+1) / 2 in an int looks like the whole answer. At n = 105 that product is five billion and the 32-bit cell wraps.
    Do the arithmetic in a long. The template tab already does; copy that, not a whiteboard int.

Interview follow-ups

Prove Kasai's k--.

Suppose suffix i shares h characters with the suffix that sits immediately before it in the suffix array. Delete the first character of both: the leftovers are suffix i+1 and "that predecessor, plus one", and they share h-1 characters. The true sorted predecessor of suffix i+1 is some suffix that is at least as large as "predecessor plus one", so its LCP with i+1 is at least h-1. You start the next extend from that leftover rather than from zero, and you only ever add more matching characters, never fewer. That is why decrementing cannot under-report and why restarting from zero is only wasted work.

SA vs suffix automaton for distinct substrings?

Both can answer in linear time after a linear (or n log n) build. The suffix array needs Kasai and the formula n(n+1)/2 - sum lcp. A suffix automaton is a DAG whose states already are the distinct slices, and the answer is a sum of their lengths. The automaton is nicer if characters arrive online. The suffix array is nicer if you also want LCP queries, a k-th slice, or a range-minimum structure on those heights.

How do you get LCP of two arbitrary suffixes?

Look up their ranks in the inverse array, then take the range-minimum of lcp strictly between those two ranks. A sparse table makes that one combine of two power-of-two blocks, which is constant time, because minimum is idempotent. Do not include lcp at the left rank itself; that slot is the share with the previous neighbour, not with the other suffix you asked about.

Why not always SA-IS?

SA-IS is linear and long, and a mistyped induced sort is a silent wrong permutation. Doubling is about forty lines, it matches the listing on this page, and it passes n = 105 in Java without drama. Learn SA-IS when the extra log factor actually bites, or when you are implementing a library rather than a contest solution.
