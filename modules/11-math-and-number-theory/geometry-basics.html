Geometry Basics

Points are long pairs. Orientation is a cross product. Almost every elementary geometry test — left of a line, segment intersection, polygon area — is that one signed area.

Hard 28 min read Prerequisites: Complexity Analysis geometry cross product orientation P2

Why this exists

You are given three points A, B, and C with integer coordinates, and you must say whether walking A to B to C turns left, turns right, or stays on a straight line. Sine and cosine will not help: they are floating, they need an angle, and contests almost never give you one. What you need is the sign of one 2-D cross product, because that sign is which way the turn goes.

Coordinates go up to 109, so a product of two differences is a 2 × 109 × 2 × 109 = 4 × 1018 candidate. That fits in a long and does not fit in an int. Division is the enemy: dividing by two to get an area, or taking a hypotenuse, throws away the exact predicate and then you compare a double to zero with an epsilon. Prefer comparing the raw cross product to zero.

Almost every elementary test is that same signed area. Two segments intersect when the endpoints of each straddle the other line, which is four turn tests. Polygon area is the shoelace sum of consecutive crosses, which computes twice the signed area of the polygon. Point-in-polygon is winding or ray-crossing, both built from the same orientation. Convex hull on the next page is a stack of left-turn tests.

The constraint that names this page is integer coordinates, often |x|, |y| ≤ 109, next to questions about left-of-a-line, intersection, or area, with n ≤ 105 so each test must be constant time. If the statement wants a floating intersection point and allows 10-9 error, still decide the combinatorial questions with long predicates first.

The 2-D cross product leftover of u.x*v.y - u.y*v.x computes twice the signed area of the triangle from the origin through u and v. Its sign is which way the turn goes: positive is left, negative is right, zero is collinear. Overflow of that multiply is the usual wrong answer.

Constraint tell: Coordinates satisfy |x|, |y| ≤ 109, so every cross product must be a long. Coordinates up to 1018 need BigInteger or a 128-bit emulation. An O(n) scan is fine at n ≤ 105; closest pair is the O(n log n) follow-up. A double epsilon test at these magnitudes is a wrong-answer factory.

Core idea and the invariant

A point is a pair of longs. Subtracting b from a is the vector from b to a. The 2-D cross product leftover of u.x*v.y - u.y*v.x computes twice the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by u and v. Orientation of A, B, C is the sign of that cross on the vectors B-A and C-A: positive means C is to the left of the directed line AB (you turn left at B), negative means you turn right, and zero means the three points are collinear. That sign is which way the turn goes, and it is the only predicate most of this page needs.

Two segments AB and CD intersect properly when C and D lie on opposite sides of AB and A and B lie on opposite sides of CD — four orientation tests whose signs oppose in pairs. Inclusive intersection also accepts the collinear-and-touching case: the orientation is zero and the shared point lies inside the bounding box of the other segment. Polygon area is the shoelace sum: add leftover of pi × pi+1 around the closed chain, which computes twice the signed area; positive means the vertices were listed counter-clockwise.

Walk A = (0, 0), B = (2, 0), C = (1, 2). The vectors are B-A = (2, 0) and C-A = (1, 2). The cross leftover of 2*2 - 0*1 = 4 is positive, so C is left of AB and you turn left at B. If C were (1, -2) the cross would be -4, a right turn. If C were (3, 0) the cross is 0, collinear, and C is not on the segment because 3 sits outside the bounding box of AB. Absolute leftover of 4 over 2 is the triangle area 2. Shoelace on a larger polygon is that same consecutive cross, summed and wrapped from last to first.

Walking A to B to C, leftover of the cross of B-A with C-A is positive exactly when you turn left at B, negative when you turn right, and zero when you do not turn. Shoelace leftover of the sum of consecutive crosses is twice the signed area of the polygon, positive when the vertices run counter-clockwise.

In plain words, you never compute an angle; you compute which way you turned, and that one sign decides left, right, collinear, intersection, and area.

Interview sentence: “The cross-product sign is which way the turn goes.”

Sign first, magnitude second

The sign of the cross tells you the turn. The absolute value is twice the area of the triangle. Overflow of the multiply corrupts both, so the long cast is not optional decoration.

Do not divide to test zero

Comparing leftover of the raw cross to 0 is exact. Dividing by 2 or taking hypot and then comparing a double to 1e-9 is how collinear points become “almost collinear” and the judge disagrees.

Structure

Algorithm

  1. Store every coordinate as a long. An int cross of two 109 differences wraps, and both the turn sign and the area become garbage.
  2. Orientation is the sign of one cross. Leftover of cross(B-A, C-A) is which way the turn goes: positive left, negative right, zero collinear. Use Long.signum.
  3. On-segment needs collinear plus a box. A point sits on AB only when the turn is zero and both coordinates lie between the endpoints, inclusive.
  4. Intersection is four turns, plus the touching case. Proper crossing is opposite signs on each pair of endpoints. Inclusive also accepts collinear-and-on-segment.
  5. Shoelace sums consecutive crosses. Wrap the last vertex to the first; leftover of that sum is twice the signed area of the polygon.
  6. A point is inside a convex polygon when every edge turns the same way. Treat a zero orientation as boundary and read whether the statement wants the boundary included.

public class Orient {

Orientation, on-segment, inclusive segment intersection, shoelace.

JavaGeom.java
public class Geom {
        static class P {
            long x, y;
            P(long x, long y) { this.x = x; this.y = y; }
            P sub(P o) { return new P(x - o.x, y - o.y); }
        }
        static long cross(P a, P b) { return a.x * b.y - a.y * b.x; }
        static int orient(P a, P b, P c) { return Long.signum(cross(b.sub(a), c.sub(a))); }
        static boolean onSeg(P a, P b, P p) {
            if (orient(a, b, p) != 0) return false;
            return Math.min(a.x, b.x) <= p.x && p.x <= Math.max(a.x, b.x)
                && Math.min(a.y, b.y) <= p.y && p.y <= Math.max(a.y, b.y);
        }
        static boolean intersect(P a, P b, P c, P d) {
            int o1 = orient(a, b, c), o2 = orient(a, b, d);
            int o3 = orient(c, d, a), o4 = orient(c, d, b);
            if (o1 != o2 && o3 != o4) return true;
            return (o1 == 0 && onSeg(a, b, c)) || (o2 == 0 && onSeg(a, b, d))
                || (o3 == 0 && onSeg(c, d, a)) || (o4 == 0 && onSeg(c, d, b));
        }
        static long area2(P[] p) {
            long s = 0;
            for (int i = 0, n = p.length; i < n; i++) s += cross(p[i], p[(i + 1) % n]);
            return s;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            P a = new P(0, 0), b = new P(2, 0), c = new P(1, 2);
            System.out.println(orient(a, b, c));
            System.out.println(area2(new P[] {a, b, c}));
        }
        // Input : triangle (0,0)(2,0)(1,2)
        // Output: 1
        //         4
    }

Point in a CCW convex polygon, including boundary.

JavaPointInConvex.java
public class PointInConvex {
        static class P { long x, y; P(long x, long y) { this.x = x; this.y = y; } }
        static long cross(P a, P b, P c) {
            return (b.x - a.x) * (c.y - a.y) - (b.y - a.y) * (c.x - a.x);
        }
        static boolean inside(P[] h, P q) {
            int n = h.length;
            for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)
                if (cross(h[i], h[(i + 1) % n], q) < 0) return false;
            return true;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            P[] h = { new P(0, 0), new P(4, 0), new P(4, 3), new P(0, 3) };
            System.out.println(inside(h, new P(2, 1)));
            System.out.println(inside(h, new P(5, 1)));
        }
        // Input : square [0,4]x[0,3], query (2,1) then (5,1)
        // Output: true
        //         false
    }

Complexity

Time O(1) per predicate; O(n) area / point-in-polygon Space O(1) extra

One orientation is two subtractions and two multiplies, all in long. Inclusive segment intersection is four orientations plus a bounding-box test, still a handful of operations. Shoelace walks each vertex once, so leftover of twice the area is O(n). Point-in-convex is one turn per edge, also O(n), or O(log n) after you binary-search the fan from a vertex.

Putting real numbers in: at n = 105 a scan of orientations is a few hundred thousand multiplies, a millisecond. Closest pair and convex hull add a sort and become the next page. A double atan2 sort at coordinate 109 will mis-order a collinear triple and then every later predicate that trusted that order is wrong.

Edge cases and pitfalls

  • int cross
    A product of two 109 differences wraps an int, so the turn sign flips or becomes zero and a left turn looks collinear. The code still compiles.
    Every coordinate and every product is a long. Print leftover of the cross of (109, 0) with (0, 109) and expect a huge positive, not a wrap.
  • Comparing doubles of areas
    Dividing the cross by 2, or taking a hypotenuse, and then comparing a double to zero with 1e-9 looks like the textbook area test and then fails on a collinear triple the judge considers exact.
    Compare leftover of the raw cross to 0. No division, no epsilon, on integer input.
  • Collinear overlap missed
    The proper-straddle test is true only for an interior crossing, so two collinear overlapping segments report false and an inclusive statement marks you wrong.
    When any orientation is zero, also accept the case where an endpoint lies on the other segment (bounding box plus collinear).
  • CW vs CCW polygon
    Shoelace leftover is negative on a clockwise listing, so a point-in-convex that demands a left turn against every edge rejects every interior point.
    Reverse the vertex list if twice-area is negative, or accept one consistent sign for the whole walk.
  • On-boundary policy
    The statement says strictly inside and you counted a point on an edge as inside, or the reverse. The turn is zero, so both policies compile and only the statement distinguishes them.
    Treat a zero orientation as its own case. Read once whether the boundary is included, and test a point that sits on an edge.

Interview follow-ups

Why not atan2 to sort by angle?

You can, with a lot of care around the branch cut and around ties. Safer: sort by quadrant first, then by leftover of the cross product, so the order is an exact integer predicate. atan2 returns a double, and two nearly-collinear vectors at coordinate 109 can come back in the wrong order.

Dot vs cross?

Leftover of the dot product u.x*v.x + u.y*v.y is |u||v|cos, so its sign is acute versus obtuse. Leftover of the cross is twice the signed area, so its sign is which way the turn goes. Length squared is leftover of dot(u, u) — you do not need a square root until the statement asks for a Euclidean length as output.

How do you test collinear in a degenerate triangle?

The three points are collinear exactly when leftover of the orientation cross is 0, which is also when the triangle area is 0. Do not introduce an epsilon on integer input. If the coordinates are 64-bit and the product might overflow a long, that is a BigInteger problem, not a floating-point one.

Point in non-convex polygon?

Shoot a ray to +∞ along x and count how many edges it crosses; odd means inside. Only count an edge that strictly straddles the ray in y, so a vertex sitting on the ray is not counted twice. The winding number, summing leftover of orientations around the boundary, is the other exact version and uses the same turn test.
