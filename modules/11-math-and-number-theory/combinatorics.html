Combinatorics

nCr = n!/(r!(n-r)!) in a prime field is three factorials. Lucas handles n ≥ p. Stars-and-bars is C(n+k-1, k-1).

Medium 28 min read Prerequisites: Modular Arithmetic nCr Lucas Catalan P1

Why this exists

You are asked how many ways there are to choose 3 toppings out of 10, or how many ways to walk from the top-left of a grid to the bottom-right using only right and down steps. Both answers are binomial coefficients C(n, r) — the number of ways to choose r items from n when order does not matter. Computing that by looping over subsets is exponential. When the answer is needed modulo a prime, a table of factorials and their inverses makes every later choose an array lookup.

The table only works while n is smaller than the prime M. Once n reaches M, leftover of n! is 0 and every choose that uses it looks like 0. Then you either multiply a short falling factorial (when r is tiny) or apply Lucas: write n and r in base p and multiply a choose of each digit pair. The phrase “n up to 1018, modulus a prime, many queries” is the constraint that picks which of those three tools you reach for.

Four named shapes sit on top of cheap nCr. Stars-and-bars counts non-negative solutions of x1+...+xk = n. Giving each bin one item first handles “at least one”. Catalan numbers count balanced brackets, binary trees, and monotone paths that stay under the diagonal. Derangements count permutations with no fixed point and belong with inclusion-exclusion on the next-but-one page. The algebra is short once choose is cheap.

In a real statement you will see “number of ways modulo 109+7” next to n ≤ 106 (build the table), or n ≤ 1018 with a small r (falling), or a small prime p with a huge n (Lucas). Pascal's triangle by dynamic programming dies at those sizes: n*r at a million is a trillion cells.

When M is prime and n < M, leftover of C(n, r) is three table lookups: leftover of n! times the inverse of r! times the inverse of (n-r)!. Precompute those two tables once. Lucas is the same choose, applied to base-p digits, for the case n ≥ M.

Recognise this pattern

Say yes when you see

  • Combinations / distributions modulo a prime
  • Stars-and-bars: n identical items, k labelled bins
  • Catalan: brackets, BSTs, monotone paths under the diagonal
  • n ≤ 1e18, r ≤ 1e6 — falling factorial
  • nCr mod p with n ≥ p — Lucas

Not this pattern when

  • Composite modulus that is not a prime power → CRT + per-prime fact
  • Exact huge integer, not a residue → BigInteger
  • Order matters and you used nCr instead of nPr
  • Subset DP is smaller than the closed form you are forcing

Constraint tell: The modulus M is a prime. A factorial table is comfortable at n ≤ 106 (a few milliseconds and a few megabytes). Falling factorial is the tool when r ≤ 106 and n is huge. Lucas needs a small-choose helper for arguments less than p, so p ≤ 106 with a huge n is the usual pairing. You cannot build a table of length 109+7.

Core idea and the invariant

Build fact[0..n] by fact[i] = fact[i-1] * i leftover M, starting at fact[0] = 1. Then invert the top cell once with Fermat, invF[n] = pow(fact[n], M-2), and walk down: invF[i-1] = invF[i] * i. That walk computes every inverse factorial from one inverse. Leftover of C(n, r) is then fact[n] * invF[r] * invF[n-r], or 0 if r is negative or larger than n. Stars-and-bars says the number of non-negative integer solutions of x1+...+xk = n is C(n+k-1, k-1) — that choose counts how to place k-1 bars among n stars and those bars.

Lucas handles n ≥ p. While n or r is nonzero, multiply leftover of C(n % p, r % p) (which is 0 if that digit of r exceeds the digit of n), then divide both by p. Each digit choose uses the small table. Catalan number Cn counts the shapes listed above and equals leftover of C(2n, n) times the inverse of n+1. If the prime divides n+1 that inverse does not exist; use the difference C(2n, n) - C(2n, n-1), which computes the same integer without dividing by n+1.

Walk a few values. Pascal's row for n = 5 is 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1: leftover of C(5, 2) is 10 because 5*4 / 2 = 10, and C(5, 3) equals C(5, 2) by symmetry. Three identical sweets into two labelled kids with empty allowed is stars-and-bars C(3+2-1, 2-1) = C(4, 1) = 4. Catalan of 3 is leftover of C(6, 3) / 4 = 20/4 = 5. Lucas on C(10, 3) modulo 7 writes 10 as digits (1, 3) and 3 as (0, 3), then leftover of C(1, 0) * C(3, 3) is 1.

Pascal's recurrence C(n, r) = C(n-1, r) + C(n-1, r-1) holds in the integers and leftover a prime. Lucas is that recurrence read in base p: leftover of (1+x)n factors as a product of leftover of (1+x) raised to each base-p digit, so the coefficient of xr is the product of the digit-wise chooses.

In plain words, you never enumerate subsets; you look up factorials, or you look up a handful of tiny chooses of digits, and those leftovers are the number of ways.

Interview sentence: “Table when n is below the prime; Lucas on the digits when it is not.”

fact[n] is 0 once n reaches M

Every later nCr that multiplies by that slot becomes leftover 0, including cases where the true choose is not a multiple of M. That is the cue to switch to Lucas, not a bug in the multiply.

Stars-and-bars chooses the bars

The number of non-negative solutions of x1+...+xk = n is C(n+k-1, k-1), not C(n+k-1, k). You are choosing positions for k-1 bars. Give each bin one item first to enforce “at least one”: leftover of C(n-1, k-1) when n ≥ k.

Structure

Algorithm

  1. Pick the tool from n, r, and M. Table if n < M and many queries; falling factorial if r is small; Lucas if n ≥ p. The wrong tool either zeros every answer or times out.
  2. Build fact and invFact when n is below M. One forward pass of multiplies, one Fermat inverse at the top, one walk down. Every later choose is three lookups.
  3. Use a falling product when r is small. Multiply leftover of (n-i) and the inverse of (i+1) for i from 0 to r-1. That computes C(n, r) without a table of length n.
  4. Apply Lucas when n reaches the prime. Product of leftover of C of each base-p digit pair, zero if any digit of r exceeds the matching digit of n.
  5. Translate distributions to stars-and-bars. Non-negative solutions of a sum is C(n+k-1, k-1); subtract the overflowing subsets with inclusion-exclusion when bins have caps.
  6. Read Catalan from a choose. Leftover of C(2n, n) times the inverse of n+1, or the difference C(2n, n) - C(2n, n-1) when the prime divides n+1.

Java implementation

JavaNcrPascal.java
public class NcrPascal {
        static int nCr(int n, int r) {
            if (r < 0 || r > n) return 0;
            int[] dp = new int[r + 1];
            dp[0] = 1;
            for (int i = 1; i <= n; i++)
                for (int j = Math.min(i, r); j > 0; j--) dp[j] += dp[j - 1];
            return dp[r];
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(nCr(5, 2));
        }
        // Input : 5 choose 2
        // Output: 10
    }
JavaNcrMod.java
public class NcrMod {
        static final int M = 1_000_000_007;
        static long[] fact, invF;
        static long modPow(long a, long e) {
            long r = 1;
            for (; e > 0; e >>= 1, a = a * a % M) if ((e & 1) == 1) r = r * a % M;
            return r;
        }
        static void build(int n) {
            fact = new long[n + 1]; invF = new long[n + 1];
            fact[0] = 1;
            for (int i = 1; i <= n; i++) fact[i] = fact[i - 1] * i % M;
            invF[n] = modPow(fact[n], M - 2);
            for (int i = n; i > 0; i--) invF[i - 1] = invF[i] * i % M;
        }
        static long nCr(int n, int r) {
            if (r < 0 || r > n) return 0;
            return fact[n] * invF[r] % M * invF[n - r] % M;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            build(20);
            System.out.println(nCr(10, 3));
            System.out.println(nCr(6, 3) * modPow(4, M - 2) % M);
        }
        // Input : C(10,3) ; Catalan_3
        // Output: 120
        //         5
    }
JavaLucas.java
public class Lucas {
        static long inv(long a, int p) {
            long r = 1, e = p - 2;
            for (a %= p; e > 0; e >>= 1, a = a * a % p) if ((e & 1) == 1) r = r * a % p;
            return r;
        }
        static long nCrSmall(int n, int r, int p) {
            if (r < 0 || r > n) return 0;
            long a = 1, b = 1;
            for (int i = 0; i < r; i++) { a = a * (n - i) % p; b = b * (i + 1) % p; }
            return a * inv(b, p) % p;
        }
        static long lucas(long n, long r, int p) {
            long ans = 1;
            while (n > 0 || r > 0) {
                ans = ans * nCrSmall((int) (n % p), (int) (r % p), p) % p;
                n /= p; r /= p;
            }
            return ans;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(lucas(10, 3, 7));
        }
        // Input : C(10,3) mod 7
        // Output: 1
    }

Complexity

Time O(n + log M) preprocess, O(1) nCr; Lucas O(log_p n) with a p-table Space O(n)

Building fact is one multiply per integer up to n, plus one Fermat inverse and a walk down for invF, so preprocess is O(n + log M) and every later choose is a handful of multiplies. Falling factorial is r multiplies and r inverses, about r log M if you invert each denominator separately. Lucas does one small choose per base-p digit, and there are O(log_p n) digits.

Putting real numbers in: a table to n = 106 is a million multiplies, a few milliseconds, then a million queries are free. Pascal DP at the same n with r = 105 is 1011 additions. Lucas on n = 1018 with p = 106+3 is a handful of digits, each a falling choose below p. You cannot allocate fact[109+7].

Edge cases and pitfalls

  • fact[n] when n >= M
    Leftover of n! is 0, so every later choose that multiplies by that slot is leftover 0, including answers that are not multiples of M. The table looks built and the multiply looks right.
    Switch to Lucas (or a falling product if r is small). A self-check is C(p, 1) leftover p, which must be 0, versus C(p+1, 1), which must be leftover 1.
  • Stars-and-bars off-by-one
    C(n+k-1, k) looks symmetric and is a legal choose, but it is not the number of non-negative solutions of a k-variable sum.
    You choose positions for k-1 bars, so the count is C(n+k-1, k-1). Enumerate the four distributions of 3 sweets to 2 kids by hand.
  • Catalan inv(n+1) when p | n+1
    Fermat's inverse of a multiple of p is leftover 0, so Catalan becomes leftover 0 even though the integer Catalan number is not a multiple of p.
    Use leftover of C(2n, n) - C(2n, n-1), which computes the same integer without dividing by n+1.
  • Integer / on the falling factorial
    Writing (n-i) / (i+1) with Java / truncates in the integers, so a later leftover is not the field element you wanted.
    Multiply by the modular inverse of i+1. The running product must stay exact leftover M at every step.
  • Order vs combination
    Arrangements are nPr = n! / (n-r)!. Using nCr when order matters (or the reverse) looks off by a consistent factor that is easy to miss on small samples.
    Ask whether swapping two chosen items produces a new answer. If yes, drop the r! in the denominator; if no, keep the choose.

Interview follow-ups

Why Lucas?

Freshman's dream says leftover of (1+x)p equals leftover of 1 + xp when the modulus is prime. Raising both sides to the base-p digits of n factors leftover of (1+x)n as a product, so the coefficient of xr — which is leftover of C(n, r) — is the product of leftover of C of each digit pair. That is Lucas, and it is why a digit of r larger than the matching digit of n forces the whole answer to 0.

Each bin at least 1?

Give every bin one item first, which spends k of the n identical items, then distribute the remaining n-k with empty allowed. Stars-and-bars then says leftover of C((n-k)+k-1, k-1) = C(n-1, k-1), provided n ≥ k. If n < k the answer is 0 because you cannot give each bin one item.

Is Catalan an integer?

Yes, always, even though the formula divides by n+1. In a prime field that division is an inverse, which fails when the prime divides n+1. The difference C(2n, n) - C(2n, n-1) is the same integer and only uses subtraction, so it is the form you ship when the modulus might divide the denominator.

n=1e18, r=1e18, p=1e9+7?

You cannot build a factorial table of length 109+7, and Lucas at that prime still wants a helper for arguments up to the prime. A well-posed statement will promise a tiny r (falling factorial), a tiny prime, or a different modulus. If both n and r are huge and the prime is 109+7, you are probably missing a reduction.
