Modular Arithmetic

Work in the ring Z/MZ: add and multiply with % M, invert when gcd(a,M)=1, and never write (a-b)%M in Java without fixing a negative remainder.

Easy 28 min read Prerequisites: Complexity Analysis, Java for DSA mod inverse P0

Why this exists

You are given two integers that can each be as large as a billion, and you must report their product, except the judge only wants the leftover after dividing by 1 000 000 007. Multiplying those two numbers already overflows a 32-bit int, and a loop that adds one of them a billion times is far too slow. Working modulo M means you keep only the remainder when you divide by M, so every intermediate value stays between 0 and M-1 and the leftover you hand back is exactly the answer the statement asked for.

The modulus in a contest is almost always a prime such as 109+7 or 998244353. A prime modulus is special because every leftover except 0 has a modular inverse — the number you multiply it by to get leftover 1, which is what division means in this world. Composite moduli lose some inverses, and then you need Euler's totient or the Chinese Remainder Theorem from later pages. The phrase “print the answer modulo 109+7” sitting next to n up to 106 or an exponent up to 1018 is the constraint that signals this page.

Java's remainder operator is not the mathematical leftover. Evaluating (1-4)%5 gives -3, not 2, because the sign of % follows the left operand. Hashing, prefix differences, and any a-b all hit this, and a negative hash becomes an ArrayIndexOutOfBounds or a missed equality. The one-liner x %= M; if (x < 0) x += M; belongs in muscle memory, and you should apply it after every subtraction before you store or compare the value.

When you need to divide, you do not write a/b in Java. The modular inverse of b is the number that acts like “one over b”: multiply a by that number and you have divided. When M is prime, Fermat's little theorem says the leftover of bM-2 is exactly that inverse, and binary exponentiation computes it in about thirty multiplies for M around 109. The next pages add sieves, Euler's totient, and the Chinese Remainder Theorem for the cases Fermat cannot cover.

Keep every value as its leftover modulo M. Add and multiply, then reduce; subtract by adding M first so Java cannot hand you a negative. Division is multiply by the modular inverse — the number that acts like one-over — and Fermat produces that inverse when M is prime.

Recognise this pattern

Say yes when you see

  • Binomial coefficients mod a prime
  • Prefix hashes and differences in a modular ring

Constraint tell: M is usually 109+7 (a 32-bit prime) or 998244353 (an NTT-friendly prime). A product of two residues fits in a 64-bit long before you reduce. Exponents go up to 1018, so a naive loop is impossible and you need about log2(1018) ≈ 60 modular multiplies. If you drop to int for the multiply, 109 × 109 wraps and the leftover is garbage.

Core idea and the invariant

Store every residue in a long. The helper add(a, b) writes a+b and, if the sum reached M, subtracts M once — that computes the leftover of the sum without calling %. The helper sub(a, b) writes a-b and, if the difference went negative, adds M once, which is the leftover of the difference. The helper mul(a, b) is (a * b) % M, and both factors must already be reduced so the product is at most about 1018 and fits in a long.

The modular inverse of a is the number inv such that the leftover of a * inv is 1 — it is the number that acts like division, because the leftover of b * inv is what “b / a” means in this ring. When M is prime and a is not a multiple of M, Fermat's little theorem says the leftover of aM-2 equals that inverse. Binary exponentiation computes the power: start with result 1, square the base, and multiply into the result on every 1-bit of the exponent. That is about thirty multiplies for M-2 when M is 109+7.

Walk leftovers modulo 5. Adding 3 and 4 gives 7, leftover 2. Subtracting 1-4 gives -3; Java's % leaves -3, then adding 5 yields 2, and the check 4+2 = 6 leftover 1 confirms it. The inverse of 3 is 2 because 3*2 = 6 leftover 1; Fermat agrees, because the leftover of 35-2 = 27 is also 2. Dividing 4 by 3 is therefore the leftover of 4*2 = 8, which is 3. Raising 2 to the 7th power: 128 leftover 3, and the binary bits of 7 (which is 4+2+1) multiply leftovers 16, 4, and 2 to the same 3.

Every value you store is a residue class modulo M: two integers that differ by a multiple of M are treated as the same. The inverse of a exists exactly when gcd(a, M) = 1 — they share no prime factor — and for prime M that is every a whose leftover is not 0.

In plain words, you never keep the full integer; you keep the leftover, you add M after a subtraction so the leftover stays non-negative, and “divide by a” means “multiply by the number that turns a into leftover 1”.

Interview sentence: “Inverse is the number that acts like division; Fermat gives it when M is prime.”

Java % is not mathematical mod

(-2) % 5 is -2 in Java, not 3. A hash or prefix difference that goes negative then looks like a different residue, so two equal keys miss each other. Always write x %= M; if (x < 0) x += M; after a subtraction.

Fermat needs a prime

The leftover of aM-2 is the inverse of a only when M is prime and a is not a multiple of M. On a composite modulus use extended Euclid from the next-but-one page, or factor M and apply Euler / CRT.

Normalise, then multiply, then invert

Algorithm

  1. Store every residue in a long. When M is around 109 a product of two residues needs 64 bits, so an int wrap before % silently destroys the leftover.
  2. Add by reducing the sum. With a and b already in 0..M-1, compute a+b and subtract M if it reached M; that leftover of the sum always fits in a long.
  3. Subtract by adding M first. Write (a - b + M) % M (or add M when the difference is negative) so Java cannot return a negative remainder that later hashes or indexes wrongly.
  4. Multiply in long, then reduce. Cast at least one factor: (long) a * b % M computes the leftover of the product; skipping the cast overflows int and the leftover is garbage.
  5. Raise to a power by binary exponentiation. Square the base and multiply into the result on every 1-bit of the exponent, about log e modular multiplies, which is how you handle exponents up to 1018.
  6. Invert by finding the number that acts like division. When M is prime call pow(a, M-2), which Fermat says is the leftover that multiplies a to 1; otherwise use extended Euclid from the later page.

Safe add/sub/mul/pow/inv for a prime M.

JavaModulo.java
public class Modulo {
        static final long M = 1_000_000_007L;
        static long add(long a, long b) { a += b; if (a >= M) a -= M; return a; }
        static long sub(long a, long b) { a -= b; if (a < 0) a += M; return a; }
        static long mul(long a, long b) { return (a * b) % M; }
        static long pow(long a, long e) {
            long r = 1;
            while (e > 0) {
                if ((e & 1) == 1) r = mul(r, a);
                a = mul(a, a);
                e >>= 1;
            }
            return r;
        }
        static long inv(long a) { return pow(a, M - 2); }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(sub(1, 4));
            System.out.println(mul(4, inv(3)));
        }
        // Input : 1-4 and 4/3 mod 1e9+7
        // Output: 1000000004
        //         333333338
    }

Generic a^e % m, m not necessarily prime.

JavaBinpow.java
public class Binpow {
        static long pow(long a, long e, long m) {
            a %= m;
            long r = 1 % m;
            while (e > 0) {
                if ((e & 1) == 1) r = (r * a) % m;
                a = (a * a) % m;
                e >>= 1;
            }
            return r;
        }
        public static void main(String[] args) {
            System.out.println(pow(2, 7, 5));
        }
        // Input : 2^7 mod 5
        // Output: 3
    }

Complexity

Time O(1) add/mul, O(log e) pow Space O(1)

Addition and subtraction are a handful of arithmetic operations: add or subtract, then maybe add or subtract M once. A multiply is one 64-bit product plus one remainder. Binary exponentiation does one squaring per bit of the exponent and one extra multiply per 1-bit, so an exponent e costs about 2 log2 e modular multiplies. The leftover of aM-2 that Fermat uses as the inverse is therefore one power, roughly sixty multiplies when M is 109+7.

Putting real numbers in: an exponent of 1018 is sixty bits, so about 120 modular multiplies, a few microseconds. A factorial table up to n = 106 is one million multiplies and one inverse at the top, then a walk down, which is a few milliseconds. A naive loop of 1018 multiplies is impossible; floating Math.pow rounds and is not a leftover in the ring.

Edge cases and pitfalls

  • Negative remainder
    Java % on a subtraction returns a negative leftover such as -3, which looks correct until you use it as a hash or an array index and miss an equality or throw.
    After every subtraction write x %= M; if (x < 0) x += M; and test (1-4) mod 5, which must come back 2.
  • int multiply
    A product 109 * 109 computed in int wraps before %, so the leftover looks like a plausible residue and the bug is silent.
    Cast at least one factor first: (long) a * b % M. Print mul(1_000_000_000, 1_000_000_000) and check it equals leftover 49 modulo 109+7.
  • inv(0)
    pow(0, M-2) returns 0, so a later multiply by that “inverse” silently computes leftover 0 instead of failing as a divide-by-zero.
    Guard a % M == 0 before inverting. A zero leftover has no number that multiplies it to leftover 1.
  • Fermat on composite M
    The leftover of aM-2 is the inverse only for prime M. On a composite it looks like the same one-liner and produces a number that does not multiply back to 1.
    Use extended Euclid when gcd(a, M) = 1, or factor M and apply Euler / CRT. Test that mul(a, inv(a)) is leftover 1.
  • pow(a, n) with n=1e18 written as a loop
    A loop of 1018 multiplies times out, and Math.pow uses doubles so the leftover is rounded, not exact.
    Binary exponentiation in the integer ring: square and multiply on bits. Test 27 mod 5 = 3 before trusting a huge exponent.

Interview follow-ups

Why 1e9+7?

It is a prime that fits in 32 bits, so two residues multiply inside a 64-bit long before you reduce. Contests picked it years ago and every library already has helpers for it. Prefer 998244353 when you might later run an NTT, because 998244353 - 1 is a high power of two; both moduli give every non-zero leftover an inverse.

Does (a/b)%M == (a%M)/(b%M)?

No. Java / is integer division, which throws away a remainder, not modular division. The leftover of 8 / 2 as ints is 4, but multiplying 8 by the inverse of 2 modulo 5 is leftover of 8 * 3 = 24, which is 4 in one ring and a different story in another. Always multiply by the number that acts like one-over, never use Java / on residues.

Overflow of a+b before %?

If a and b are already reduced and M is less than about 262, the sum a+b still fits in a long. For a modulus near 263 skip % and subtract M when the sum reaches it, which is also faster. Products are the ones that need the long cast; sums of two residues almost never overflow 64 bits at contest moduli.

Wilson's theorem?

Wilson says the leftover of (P-1)! is P-1 (that is, leftover -1) when P is prime. It is a cute check on a tiny prime and a terrible primality test: computing that factorial is linear in P, and Miller-Rabin is the test you actually ship. Do not invert a factorial of P or larger; that leftover is already 0.
